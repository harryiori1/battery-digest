## 01 Mercedes‑Benz and ProLogium Launch Joint Testing of Gen4 All‑Inorganic Solid‑State Cells
Mercedes‑Benz and ProLogium have signed a joint testing agreement that will put the Taiwanese firm’s latest Gen4 Superfluidized All‑Inorganic Solid‑State Battery Cell technology through a preliminary evaluation on Mercedes‑Benz platforms [Mercedes‑Benz and ProLogium Joint Testing Agreement](https://prologium.com/mercedes-benz-and-prologium-have-entered-into-a-joint-testing-agreement-including-a-preliminary-platform-evaluation-of-of-prologiums-latest-gen4-superfluidized-all-inorganic-solid-state-batt/). The partnership marks one of the most high‑profile OEM engagements with a solid‑state specialist since ProLogium’s recent mass‑production announcements, and it could accelerate the path to commercial solid‑state packs in luxury vehicles.

ProLogium’s Gen4 cells employ a fully inorganic, super‑fluidized ceramic electrolyte that eliminates the liquid electrolyte layer typical of conventional lithium‑ion packs. According to the company, the architecture delivers an energy density of up to **400 Wh kg⁻¹** at the cell level, a 30 % improvement over its earlier 381 Wh kg⁻¹ mass‑production cells. The electrolyte is a sulfide‑based ceramic that offers high ionic conductivity (>10⁻³ S cm⁻¹) while maintaining mechanical robustness, enabling thin‑film designs that can be stacked into high‑voltage modules.

The testing scope will focus on three core performance metrics:

1. **Energy‑density validation** – confirming the claimed 400 Wh kg⁻¹ under real‑world charge‑discharge cycles.  
2. **Thermal safety** – subjecting the cells to over‑charge, short‑circuit, and puncture tests to verify the intrinsic fire‑resistance of the solid electrolyte.  
3. **Cycle life** – targeting >1,000 full cycles at 80 % depth‑of‑discharge while retaining >90 % capacity retention.

Mercedes‑Benz will integrate prototype modules into a test fleet of its EQS‑II sedan and the upcoming EQE‑X concept, providing data on vehicle‑level range, charging speed, and system‑level thermal management. The automaker’s engineering team will also assess the compatibility of ProLogium’s dry‑electrode manufacturing approach with its existing cell‑assembly lines, a step that could simplify scale‑up by removing the wet‑coating step that dominates current lithium‑ion production.

Why this matters: solid‑state batteries have long been touted as the “holy grail” for EVs, promising higher energy density, faster charging, and superior safety. Yet the technology has struggled to move beyond lab‑scale demonstrations because of manufacturing complexity, electrolyte‑electrode interface stability, and cost. By involving a premium OEM early in the validation process, ProLogium gains direct feedback on integration challenges, while Mercedes‑Benz secures a potential differentiator for its next‑generation electric models. If the tests confirm the performance claims, the partnership could pave the way for limited‑run solid‑state packs in luxury vehicles as early as 2028, with broader volume production following a successful pilot.

The agreement also signals a shift in the industry’s risk appetite. After years of cautious pilot programs, major carmakers are now willing to co‑invest in solid‑state validation, suggesting that the cost curve of inorganic electrolytes and dry‑electrode processes is flattening. For investors and competitors, the collaboration underscores the strategic importance of securing supply‑chain access to high‑performance solid electrolytes—particularly sulfide‑based ceramics that combine conductivity with manufacturability.

In sum, the Mercedes‑Benz/ProLogium joint testing effort is a concrete step toward commercial solid‑state batteries in premium EVs. Its outcome will be watched closely by rivals such as QuantumScape and Solid Power, and could set the benchmark for how OEMs evaluate solid‑state technologies on real vehicles.
