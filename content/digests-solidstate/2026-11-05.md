## 01 CATL advances 20 Ah solid‑state EV cells targeting 500 Wh/kg
Chinese battery leader Contemporary Amperex Technology Co. Ltd. (CATL) announced a major milestone in its solid‑state battery programme: a 20 Ah all‑solid‑state cell that delivers an energy density of up to 500 Wh/kg. The prototype, revealed in a technical briefing on 28 October 2026, represents the largest capacity solid‑state cell demonstrated to date and moves the company a step closer to commercialising the technology for electric‑vehicle (EV) applications.

The new cell uses a sulfide‑based solid electrolyte combined with a high‑voltage nickel‑rich cathode and a lithium‑metal anode. According to CATL, the sulfide electrolyte offers ionic conductivities above 10 mS cm⁻¹ at room temperature, enabling fast charge‑discharge cycles while maintaining thermal stability. The lithium‑metal anode, protected by a thin interfacial layer, mitigates dendrite formation—a long‑standing obstacle for solid‑state designs.

Why it matters: achieving 500 Wh/kg would push solid‑state batteries well beyond the 250‑300 Wh/kg typical of today’s liquid‑electrolyte lithium‑ion packs, translating into either longer vehicle range or lighter battery packs. CATL’s 20 Ah capacity also aligns with the cell format required for high‑energy EV modules, meaning the technology could be integrated into existing vehicle architectures without a complete redesign of battery packs.

CATL plans a pilot‑scale production line in its Ningde facility for early‑2027, targeting a limited run of 5 GWh per year. The company says the pilot will focus on “high‑performance premium EVs” before scaling to mass‑market models. If the pilot meets performance and cost targets, CATL expects a full‑scale commercial launch by 2030, positioning itself to compete directly with solid‑state projects at QuantumScape and Solid Power.

The announcement also underscores a broader industry shift: several major OEMs have signalled interest in solid‑state packs for next‑generation models, and governments in Europe and China are offering subsidies for high‑energy‑density batteries. CATL’s progress could accelerate those programmes, especially as the company holds a dominant share of the global EV‑battery market.

Nevertheless, challenges remain. Scaling sulfide electrolyte production, ensuring long‑term cycle life (>1,000 cycles), and controlling manufacturing costs are still open questions. CATL’s roadmap hinges on solving these issues while maintaining the safety standards required for mass‑market vehicles.

Overall, the 20 Ah solid‑state prototype marks a tangible step from laboratory research to near‑commercial reality. If CATL can deliver on its timeline, the technology could reshape EV powertrain design, offering higher range, faster charging, and improved safety—all critical factors for the next wave of electric mobility.
