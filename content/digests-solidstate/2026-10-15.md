## 01 ProLogium Begins Mass Production of High‑Energy‑Density All‑Solid‑State Battery, Reaching 381 Wh/kg and 903 Wh/L

Taiwan‑based solid‑state pioneer ProLogium announced that it has entered mass‑production of a new all‑solid‑state lithium‑metal cell that delivers a gravimetric energy density of 381 Wh kg⁻¹ and a volumetric density of 903 Wh L⁻¹ — figures that, if sustained at scale, would eclipse most conventional lithium‑ion chemistries [ProLogium Press Release](https://prologium.com/prologium-begins-mass-production-of-high-energy-density-all-solid-state-battery-reaching-381-wh-kg-and-903-wh-l/).  

The cell uses ProLogium’s proprietary “DCN” (dual‑ceramic‑nanolayer) electrolyte, a sulfide‑based solid electrolyte that combines high ionic conductivity (>10⁻³ S cm⁻¹) with excellent chemical stability against lithium metal. By sandwiching a thin lithium‑metal anode between two ceramic layers, the design suppresses dendrite formation while maintaining low interfacial resistance. The cathode is a high‑loading nickel‑rich NCM 811 composite applied via a dry‑electrode process that eliminates the need for toxic solvents and reduces manufacturing energy by roughly 30 % compared with slurry‑coated electrodes.

Why it matters: Energy density is the primary barrier to solid‑state adoption in electric vehicles (EVs). Current commercial solid‑state prototypes hover around 250–300 Wh kg⁻¹, limiting range gains over state‑of‑the‑art lithium‑ion packs. ProLogium’s 381 Wh kg⁻¹ target translates to an estimated 30‑40 % increase in vehicle range for a comparable pack size, potentially enabling 600‑plus kilometre (≈370 mi) real‑world ranges without sacrificing safety. Moreover, the volumetric figure of 903 Wh L⁻¹ suggests that packaging constraints—particularly for compact city cars—could be relaxed.

Implications for commercialization: The announcement marks the first time a solid‑state cell with such energy metrics has moved beyond pilot lines into serial production. ProLogium plans to ship the first batches to its European partners by Q2 2027, with an initial annual capacity of 5 GWh, scalable to 30 GWh within five years. If the yield targets (≥95 % wafer‑level pass) are met, the cost per kilowatt‑hour could approach $120, a level that begins to compete with high‑nickel lithium‑ion chemistries. The move also underscores the importance of the dry‑electrode technique, which reduces solvent‑related emissions and aligns with ProLogium’s broader sustainability goals.

Industry observers note that the real test will be long‑term cycling stability. ProLogium claims >1,000 cycles with <10 % capacity fade at 0.5 C charge/discharge, but independent validation will be crucial. Should the figures hold, the company could become the first solid‑state supplier to secure high‑volume contracts with premium EV manufacturers, accelerating the transition from lithium‑ion to lithium‑metal solid‑state platforms.

## 02 ProLogium Advances Dunkirk Gigafactory as Site Infrastructure and Localization Work Accelerate

ProLogium disclosed rapid progress on its planned gigafactory in Dunkirk, northern France, reporting that site‑level infrastructure—foundations, power substations, and clean‑room modules—has reached 70 % completion and that localization of key component suppliers is on track for a Q4 2026 start‑up [ProLogium Press Release](https://prologium.com/prologium-advances-dunkirk-gigafactory-as-site-infrastructure-and-localization-work-accelerate/).  

The 150‑hectare facility is designed to produce up to 10 GWh of all‑solid‑state cells per year, with a modular layout that can be expanded to 30 GWh. Crucially, the plant will integrate a vertically‑integrated supply chain for the sulfide electrolyte, ceramic separators, and dry‑electrode cathodes, sourcing raw materials from European partners in Belgium, Germany, and the Netherlands. ProLogium has signed memoranda of understanding (MoUs) with two ceramic manufacturers to co‑develop a low‑temperature sintering process that reduces furnace energy consumption by 25 % and shortens cycle time from 48 hours to under 24 hours.

Why it matters: One of the persistent challenges for solid‑state commercialization is the lack of a localized, high‑volume component ecosystem. Most solid electrolytes are currently produced in small batches in Asia, leading to supply bottlenecks and high logistics costs. By establishing a European hub, ProLogium not only shortens lead times but also mitigates geopolitical risk—an increasingly important factor for automakers seeking supply‑chain resilience.

The factory’s design also incorporates a “dry‑process” electrode line, eliminating the traditional solvent‑based slurry coating step. This reduces water usage by an estimated 80 % and cuts volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions, aligning the plant with the EU’s Green Deal objectives. In addition, the facility will feature an on‑site recycling loop for spent electrolyte and ceramic waste, aiming for a closed‑material cycle that could lower the effective material cost by up to 15 %.

Implications for the market: The Dunkirk gigafactory positions ProLogium as the first solid‑state battery maker with a truly European‑scale production footprint. Automakers such as Renault and Stellantis have publicly expressed interest in sourcing solid‑state cells for next‑generation models, and a nearby supply source could accelerate prototype integration. Moreover, the plant’s capacity will help bridge the “valley of death” between laboratory breakthroughs and mass‑market adoption, providing the volume needed to drive down unit costs through economies of scale.

Analysts caution that the timeline remains aggressive; ramp‑up of a novel solid‑state line typically encounters yield challenges, especially with ceramic electrolyte uniformity. However, ProLogium’s partnership network and its recent entry into mass production (see story 01) suggest that the company has accumulated sufficient process knowledge to mitigate these risks. If the Dunkirk site reaches full operational status by early 2028, it could supply the burgeoning European EV market with solid‑state cells that deliver both higher energy density and the safety advantages of non‑flammable electrolytes, potentially reshaping the continent’s battery supply landscape.
