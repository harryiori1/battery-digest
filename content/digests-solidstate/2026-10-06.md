## 01 China Aims for Mass‑Produced Solid‑State Batteries by 2030, CATL Warns of Cost Hurdles
Beijing has set an ambitious goal to have mass‑produced all‑solid‑state batteries (ASSBs) on the market by 2030, a timeline that could reshape the global EV landscape if achieved. The policy push was outlined in a recent statement from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, which earmarked subsidies, research grants, and a streamlined approval process for firms that demonstrate viable solid‑state chemistries [InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/810656/china-solid-state-batteries-2030/).

The announcement comes at a time when the world’s largest lithium‑ion maker, CATL, is publicly cautioning that scaling ASSBs “at an affordable price point remains a formidable technical challenge.” CATL’s R&D chief highlighted three bottlenecks: (1) the need for high‑ionic‑conductivity sulfide or garnet electrolytes that can operate safely at room temperature, (2) the difficulty of fabricating defect‑free lithium‑metal anodes without dendrite formation, and (3) the lack of high‑throughput, dry‑electrode manufacturing lines that can replace the slurry‑casting processes used for conventional cells [InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/810656/china-solid-state-batteries-2030/).

If China can overcome these hurdles, the payoff could be dramatic. Solid‑state cells promise energy densities of 400‑500 Wh kg⁻¹—roughly 30‑40 % higher than today’s best lithium‑ion packs—while offering intrinsic safety benefits such as non‑flammability and reduced thermal runaway risk. For EV manufacturers, this translates into longer ranges (potentially 800 km on a single charge) and lighter vehicle platforms, which could accelerate the shift from internal‑combustion to electric powertrains.

However, the cost equation remains uncertain. Current laboratory‑scale ASSBs still cost upwards of $500 kWh⁻¹, compared with $120‑150 kWh⁻¹ for mature lithium‑ion chemistries. Achieving a sub‑$200 kWh⁻¹ price—often cited as the threshold for mass‑market EV adoption—will require breakthroughs in electrolyte synthesis, scalable lithium‑metal handling, and roll‑to‑roll dry‑electrode processing.

The Chinese government’s commitment signals a willingness to subsidize early‑stage risk, but industry players will need to demonstrate that the promised performance gains can be delivered at scale and at a competitive price. The next few years will likely see a surge in pilot lines, joint ventures, and intellectual‑property battles as both domestic firms and foreign entrants vie for a foothold in what could become the next generation of energy storage.
