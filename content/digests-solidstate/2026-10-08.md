## 01 ProLogium Unveils ‘DCN’ Third‑State Electrolyte at Paris Motor Show
ProLogium announced a breakthrough in solid‑electrolyte chemistry at the 2026 Paris Motor Show, introducing what it calls “DCN” – a “Third‑State” electrolyte that sits between the traditional liquid and solid phases. The company describes DCN as a super‑fluidic, glass‑like ceramic that can flow during cell assembly yet solidify into a highly conductive lattice once the battery is sealed. According to ProLogium, the material achieves an ionic conductivity of 12 mS cm⁻¹ at room temperature—roughly three times higher than its previous garnet‑type oxides and comparable to the best sulfide electrolytes, but without the moisture sensitivity that has plagued sulfide chemistries.  

The DCN electrolyte also boasts a wide electrochemical stability window (up to 5.5 V vs. Li⁺/Li) and a measured interfacial resistance of less than 10 Ω cm² when paired with ProLogium’s Gen4 lithium‑metal anode. In practical terms, these figures translate to faster charge rates (targeting 1 C full‑cell charge) and a projected energy density of 380 Wh kg⁻¹ for pouch‑type cells, a figure that would place the technology among the highest‑performing solid‑state batteries demonstrated to date.  

Why the announcement matters is twofold. First, the “third‑state” concept could resolve a long‑standing trade‑off in solid‑state manufacturing: the need for high‑temperature sintering or high‑pressure compaction versus the desire for scalable, low‑cost roll‑to‑roll processes. Because DCN can be deposited via a slurry coating and then thermally annealed at ≤200 °C, it aligns with existing dry‑electrode lines, potentially lowering capital expenditures for mass production. Second, the improved interfacial compatibility with lithium metal reduces dendrite formation risk, a critical safety hurdle for commercial deployment.  

Industry observers note that ProLogium’s move mirrors a broader shift toward hybrid electrolyte designs that blend the mechanical robustness of ceramics with the processing ease of polymers. If the DCN material lives up to its lab‑scale performance in pilot‑line cells, it could accelerate the timeline for solid‑state EVs from the current 2030 horizon to the mid‑2020s. ProLogium has indicated that first‑pilot modules using DCN will be assembled later this year, with a target of small‑volume production in 2027.  

The announcement underscores ProLogium’s strategy of leveraging proprietary electrolyte chemistry to differentiate itself from rivals such as QuantumScape and Solid Power, which remain focused on sulfide‑based systems. By delivering a high‑conductivity, process‑friendly electrolyte, ProLogium may close the gap between laboratory breakthroughs and the manufacturing scale required for automotive adoption.

## 02 ProLogium Charts Evolution of Its Lithium‑Ceramic Battery Technology
In a detailed technical retrospective published on its corporate site, ProLogium traced the development of its lithium‑ceramic solid‑state batteries from early research prototypes to the 2026 Gen4 platform that now claims 380 Wh kg⁻¹ energy density and a 1,200‑cycle lifespan. The timeline highlights three pivotal milestones: (1) the 2018 transition from sulfide‑based electrolytes to a proprietary garnet‑type oxide (LLZO) that offered superior moisture stability; (2) the 2021 introduction of a thin‑film ceramic separator that enabled a 0.2 mm cell stack thickness, dramatically improving volumetric energy; and (3) the 2024 launch of a “super‑fluidized” ceramic slurry process that allowed dry‑electrode coating at industrial speeds while preserving high ionic conductivity.  

Each stage brought measurable performance gains. The initial LLZO cells delivered 250 Wh kg⁻¹ with a modest 500‑cycle life, limited by interfacial resistance between the ceramic and lithium metal. By 2021, the thin‑film separator reduced interfacial impedance to 15 Ω cm², pushing energy density to 300 Wh kg⁻¹ and extending cycle life to 800 cycles. The 2024 slurry breakthrough, coupled with a new surface‑engineered lithium‑metal anode, slashed interfacial resistance to under 10 Ω cm² and enabled the current Gen4 cell architecture, which integrates a 30 µm lithium‑metal foil and a 150 µm ceramic electrolyte.  

The article also outlines ProLogium’s scaling strategy. The company has built a pilot line in Taiwan capable of producing 5 GWh of solid‑state cells per year, leveraging existing dry‑electrode equipment to keep capex below $1,200 per kWh—significantly lower than the $2,500‑$3,000 per kWh quoted for many sulfide‑based projects. ProLogium emphasizes that its ceramic chemistry is compatible with a “dry‑electrode” manufacturing flow, eliminating the need for solvent‑based slurry drying and reducing environmental impact.  

Why this evolution matters is that it demonstrates a viable pathway from high‑performance laboratory cells to a manufacturable product line. The incremental improvements in ionic conductivity (from 3 mS cm⁻¹ in 2018 to 12 mS cm⁻¹ in 2026) and interfacial engineering directly address the two most cited barriers to solid‑state commercialization: power density and cycle life. Moreover, the company’s focus on dry‑process compatibility could make its technology attractive to OEMs seeking to retrofit existing lithium‑ion production lines rather than build entirely new factories.  

Analysts see ProLogium’s roadmap as a template for the broader industry, suggesting that ceramic‑based solid‑state batteries may achieve commercial parity with lithium‑ion within the next five years, especially for high‑value applications such as premium EVs and aerospace. The firm’s transparent documentation of its technical milestones also provides a benchmark for competitors and investors tracking the solid‑state race.
