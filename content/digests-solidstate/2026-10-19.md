## 01 ProLogium Maps Next‑Generation Lithium‑Ceramic Battery Roadmap

ProLogium, a leading developer of all‑solid‑state lithium‑metal batteries, has published a detailed overview of its lithium‑ceramic electrolyte technology and a three‑phase roadmap aimed at reaching commercial‑grade performance by the early 2030s. The “Evolution of ProLogium’s Lithium Ceramic Battery Technology” paper, posted on the company’s website, outlines how incremental advances in electrolyte composition, interface engineering, and dry‑electrode processing will lift gravimetric energy density from the current 381 Wh kg⁻¹ to beyond 500 Wh kg⁻¹ while maintaining a cycle life of 1,000 + full‑depth cycles [ProLogium](https://prologium.com/evolution-of-prologiums-lithium-ceramic-battery-technology/)​.

**Phase 1 (2024‑2026):** ProLogium focuses on optimizing its sulfide‑based ceramic electrolyte (LLZO‑type garnet) to reduce interfacial resistance with lithium metal. By introducing a thin, conformal interlayer of lithium‑phosphorous oxynitride (LiPON), the company reports a drop in charge‑transfer resistance from 150 Ω cm² to under 30 Ω cm², enabling 1 C charge rates without dendrite formation. The phase also introduces a pilot dry‑coating line that eliminates solvent‑based slurry steps, cutting electrode‑fabrication energy use by roughly 40 % and improving coating uniformity on the brittle ceramic sheets.

**Phase 2 (2027‑2029):** The roadmap pivots to “high‑voltage” cathode integration. ProLogium is testing nickel‑rich NCM 811 particles coated with a thin Al₂O₃ layer to suppress electrolyte decomposition at voltages up to 4.6 V. Early cell prototypes demonstrate a specific energy of 470 Wh kg⁻¹ at 0.5 C, a 23 % jump over the current generation. In parallel, the company scales its roll‑to‑roll dry‑electrode line to a 500‑m width, targeting an annual output of 30 GWh of solid‑state cells.

**Phase 3 (2030‑2032):** The final stage aims for “mass‑manufacturable” 5‑Ah pouch cells with a target energy density of 520 Wh kg⁻¹ and a volumetric density of 1,200 Wh L⁻¹. ProLogium plans to integrate a proprietary “self‑healing” ceramic electrolyte that can autonomously repair micro‑cracks induced during high‑rate cycling, a claim supported by in‑situ synchrotron X‑ray tomography studies. If successful, the technology could enable electric‑vehicle ranges exceeding 800 km on a single charge while preserving the safety advantages of solid‑state chemistry.

Why it matters: The roadmap tackles three critical bottlenecks that have stalled solid‑state commercialization—interfacial resistance, high‑voltage stability, and scalable manufacturing. By coupling electrolyte advances with dry‑electrode processing, ProLogium seeks to avoid the costly solvent‑recovery steps that dominate conventional lithium‑ion production, potentially lowering the levelized cost of storage (LCOS) to a range competitive with next‑generation lithium‑ion chemistries. Moreover, the projected energy‑density milestones would place solid‑state cells ahead of most commercial lithium‑ion offerings, giving automakers a compelling safety and performance argument.

Industry observers note that ProLogium’s phased approach mirrors the “learning‑by‑doing” strategy employed by early‑stage semiconductor firms, where incremental yield improvements eventually unlock exponential cost reductions. If the company meets its Phase 2 targets, it could attract a new wave of OEM partnerships beyond the already‑announced collaborations with Mercedes‑Benz and Rimac, accelerating the transition from niche high‑performance prototypes to volume‑produced EV powertrains. The next few years will be decisive: successful scale‑up of the dry‑electrode line and validation of the high‑voltage cathode interface will be the litmus tests that determine whether ProLogium can move from laboratory breakthroughs to a commercially viable solid‑state battery platform.
