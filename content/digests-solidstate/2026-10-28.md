## 01 CATL Advances to 20 Ah All‑Solid‑State Cells Targeting 500 Wh/kg Energy Density
China’s battery behemoth CATL announced a new milestone in its solid‑state program: the development of 20 Ah all‑solid‑state (ASS) pouch cells that deliver an energy density approaching 500 Wh kg⁻¹ [Electrek](https://electrek.co/2026/10/28/catl-pushes-ahead-20-ah-solid-state-ev-batteries-500-wh-kg/). The cells employ a sulfide‑based ceramic electrolyte and a lithium‑metal anode, both of which have been the focus of intensive research over the past five years.  

The 20 Ah format is a critical size for automotive modules, allowing manufacturers to build packs with fewer cells while preserving high voltage and power output. CATL says the new cells have a gravimetric energy density 30 % higher than its best‑in‑class lithium‑ion NCM packs and can operate safely over a 0 °C to 60 °C temperature window, thanks to the inherent thermal stability of the solid electrolyte.  

Production is slated to begin on a pilot line in 2027, with an initial output of 5 GWh per year. CATL plans to scale to a full‑scale gigafactory by 2030, contingent on yield improvements and cost reductions in the sulfide electrolyte synthesis. The company estimates that the cost per kilowatt‑hour could fall below $120 once volume manufacturing is achieved, a figure that would make solid‑state packs competitive with conventional lithium‑ion for mass‑market EVs.  

Why the news matters: achieving 20 Ah cells bridges the gap between laboratory‑scale prototypes (typically 1–5 Ah) and the larger formats required for vehicle integration. It also demonstrates that CATL can translate its solid‑state chemistry into a manufacturable product, a hurdle that has stalled many rivals. If the promised energy density and safety claims hold up in real‑world testing, the technology could enable EVs with 600 km+ ranges on a single charge, faster charging (up to 200 kW), and reduced cooling system complexity.  

The announcement also intensifies competition with other solid‑state players such as QuantumScape and Solid Power, which are pursuing different chemistries (e.g., lithium‑metal‑polymer and garnet‑based oxides). CATL’s advantage lies in its massive scale, established supply chain for sulfide precursors, and close ties to Chinese automakers. The next few years will reveal whether the company can keep its timeline and cost targets, but the 20 Ah breakthrough is a clear signal that solid‑state batteries are moving from the lab toward the production line.

## 02 Xpeng Deploys Small‑Batch Solid‑State Batteries for Its Iron Humanoid Robot
Chinese EV and robotics maker Xpeng has begun limited‑volume production of solid‑state batteries to power its newly unveiled Iron humanoid robot, marking the first commercial use of ASS cells in a non‑automotive platform [CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2026/10/28/solid-state-batteries-xpeng-iron-small-batch-production/). The batteries are based on a sulfide electrolyte (Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂‑type) paired with a lithium‑metal anode, delivering 350 Wh kg⁻¹ and a discharge capability of 5 C.  

Each Iron robot is equipped with a 12 Ah pouch cell that provides up to eight hours of continuous operation under typical load conditions, or up to 30 minutes of high‑power activity such as rapid locomotion or heavy lifting. The solid electrolyte eliminates the need for liquid electrolyte management, improving safety in the robot’s compact chassis and allowing operation in temperatures ranging from –20 °C to 70 °C.  

Xpeng’s decision to adopt solid‑state chemistry stems from the robot’s demanding power‑density profile. Conventional lithium‑ion packs would either add excessive weight or require bulky cooling systems, both of which compromise the robot’s agility and aesthetic design. By using a solid‑state cell, Xpeng reduces the battery pack mass by roughly 15 % and removes the liquid‑cooling loop, simplifying the overall mechanical architecture.  

The production run is limited to 500 units, intended as a field‑test to validate long‑term cycle life (targeting 1,000 cycles) and to gather data on degradation mechanisms unique to sulfide electrolytes under dynamic mechanical stress. Xpeng plans to feed the results into a second‑generation cell that could reach 400 Wh kg⁻¹ and support larger robotic platforms, such as warehouse automation units.  

From an industry perspective, Xpeng’s move demonstrates that solid‑state batteries are finding niche applications beyond passenger vehicles, where the value of safety, energy density, and form‑factor flexibility outweighs the current cost premium. It also highlights the growing ecosystem of suppliers capable of delivering sulfide electrolytes at a scale sufficient for limited‑batch production—a capability that was scarce just two years ago. If the Iron robot’s performance meets expectations, it could accelerate adoption of solid‑state power sources in other robotics, aerospace, and portable‑power markets, providing a new revenue stream for solid‑state manufacturers and further de‑risking the technology for broader automotive use.
