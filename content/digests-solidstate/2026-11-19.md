## 01 QuantumScape Unveils Osprey All‑Solid‑State Cell with 1,000 Cycles at 1 atm

QuantumScape announced that its next‑generation “Osprey” platform has achieved a full‑cell cycle life of 1,000 cycles to 80 % depth‑of‑discharge (DoD) while operating at ambient pressure (1 atm). The breakthrough replaces the liquid electrolyte in the cathode with a sulfide‑based solid electrolyte, a move that has long been touted as the key to unlocking high‑energy‑density, safe lithium‑metal batteries for electric vehicles (EVs) and other high‑performance applications.

The Osprey cell delivers an energy density of roughly 500 Wh kg⁻¹ (based on active material mass) and a power capability of 2 kW kg⁻¹, according to QuantumScape’s technical brief. Most importantly, the cell no longer requires the heavy mechanical clamping that has plagued earlier solid‑state prototypes. Traditional sulfide‑electrolyte designs need pressures of 150–300 psi to maintain intimate contact between the solid electrolyte, cathode, and lithium‑metal anode, adding weight, cost, and manufacturing complexity. By engineering a composite cathode architecture that self‑maintains interfacial contact, QuantumScape claims the Osprey can be produced in a conventional roll‑to‑roll cell‑stacking line without specialized pressure equipment.

Why this matters: Cycle life has been the Achilles’ heel of solid‑state batteries. Earlier lab‑scale cells often showed impressive specific energy but degraded after a few hundred cycles, especially when paired with lithium metal. Achieving 1,000 cycles at 1 atm demonstrates that the interfacial stability of the sulfide electrolyte (likely a lithium‑thiophosphate formulation) is sufficient to suppress dendrite formation and electrolyte decomposition over realistic usage patterns. This milestone narrows the performance gap between solid‑state and today’s lithium‑ion chemistries, which typically offer 1,500–2,000 cycles but at lower energy density and with safety trade‑offs.

QuantumScape’s roadmap now targets pilot‑scale production of Osprey cells by 2028, with a goal of integrating the technology into a commercial EV platform by 2030. The company says it has already qualified the electrolyte for >5 kWh of cell capacity in a single stack, a scale that would support a 400‑km range vehicle with a modestly sized pack. If the pressure‑free manufacturing claim holds up at scale, the cost advantage could be significant: eliminating high‑pressure tooling reduces capital expenditure by an estimated 30 % and simplifies supply‑chain logistics.

Industry observers note that the Osprey results could accelerate partnerships with automakers seeking to differentiate their EVs with longer range and faster charging. The solid‑state cell’s ability to operate safely at higher voltages (up to 4.5 V vs. LiCoO₂) also opens the door to faster charge rates without the thermal runaway risk associated with liquid electrolytes. However, challenges remain, including scaling the thin‑film lithium‑metal anode and ensuring long‑term calendar life under real‑world temperature swings.

Overall, QuantumScape’s Osprey platform represents a tangible step toward the commercial viability of all‑solid‑state batteries. By delivering a high‑cycle‑life, pressure‑free cell, the company addresses two of the most critical barriers—manufacturability and durability—bringing the industry closer to the promise of safer, higher‑energy EV powertrains. Further validation in pilot factories and third‑party testing will be essential, but the announcement marks a noteworthy inflection point for solid‑state technology.
