## 01 Hyundai Targets Small‑Volume Cars with Solid‑State Batteries by 2029
Hyundai Motor Group’s head of research and development, Manfred Harrer, told InsideEVs that the automaker is betting on solid‑state batteries (SSBs) for a limited‑run of premium models slated for launch no later than 2029. The timeline is aggressive: Harrer expects a “small‑volume” production line to be operational within five years, with an initial output of roughly 30,000 cells per year.  

The company is pursuing a sulfide‑based solid electrolyte, which promises energy densities of 500 Wh kg⁻¹ and a charge‑rate capability of up to 5 C (equivalent to a full charge in under 12 minutes). Hyundai’s internal testing reportedly shows a capacity fade of less than 5 % after 1,000 cycles at 3 C, a figure that would outpace most current lithium‑ion chemistries.  

Hyundai is not developing the technology in isolation. The automaker has signed a strategic partnership with a Japanese solid‑electrolyte specialist, aiming to co‑manufacture the electrolyte sheets at a new pilot line in Ulsan. The collaboration also includes joint IP filing, with the first patents expected to be published in early 2027.  

Why does this matter? If Hyundai can bring SSBs to market on the promised schedule, it would be the first major OEM to commercialise the technology at scale, potentially forcing rivals such as Toyota, BMW and Volkswagen to accelerate their own roadmaps. The move also signals confidence that the remaining technical hurdles—namely dendrite formation and cost‑effective mass production—are being overcome.  

From a market perspective, a limited‑run SSB vehicle could command a price premium of $10,000–$15,000 over comparable lithium‑ion models, giving Hyundai a new revenue stream while testing consumer acceptance. The announcement also aligns with South Korea’s broader push for advanced battery manufacturing, which the government backs with subsidies targeting a 30 % reduction in battery pack cost by 2030.  

Overall, Hyundai’s five‑year target underscores a shift from laboratory proof‑of‑concept to commercial viability, a transition that could reshape the premium EV segment and set a new benchmark for energy density, safety, and charging speed. ## 02 Geely Unveils ‘Battery Healing’ Fast‑Charging Technology in New Podcast Reveal
In the latest Electrek podcast episode (September 25), Geely Auto disclosed a novel “battery‑healing” technology that it claims can mitigate the wear typically associated with ultra‑fast charging. The system combines a proprietary electrolyte additive with a real‑time cell‑balancing algorithm, allowing the battery pack to absorb a 4‑minute, 800 kW charge without the usual 20‑30 % capacity loss seen after 300 cycles at comparable rates.  

Geely’s engineering team reports that the additive forms a protective solid‑electrolyte interphase (SEI) that self‑reconstructs during each high‑current pulse, effectively “healing” micro‑cracks that would otherwise propagate and cause irreversible degradation. In bench tests, a 75 kWh pack equipped with the technology retained 96 % of its original capacity after 500 fast‑charge cycles, compared with 82 % for a conventional lithium‑ion pack under the same conditions.  

The company says the technology is ready for integration into its upcoming flagship sedan, slated for a 2027 launch in the Chinese market. Production‑scale implementation will involve a modest redesign of the pack’s cooling architecture to accommodate the additive’s temperature‑sensitive chemistry, but Geely assures that the change will not affect the vehicle’s overall cost structure.  

Industry analysts view the announcement as a potential game‑changer for the fast‑charging ecosystem, where range‑anxiety and battery longevity have been persistent pain points. If Geely’s claims hold up in real‑world use, the technology could reduce the total cost of ownership for EV owners by extending battery life and enabling widespread deployment of 4‑minute “hyper‑charge” stations.  

The broader implication is a shift in the fast‑charging value proposition: rather than merely delivering speed, manufacturers may now need to demonstrate that rapid charging does not compromise durability. Geely’s approach could spur other OEMs and cell makers to explore similar electrolyte‑additive strategies, accelerating the industry’s move toward truly ultra‑fast, long‑lasting EVs.
