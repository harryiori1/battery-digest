## 01 Janus Electric Secures Electrovaya Battery‑Swap Pack Supply for Class 8 Trucks
Australian retro‑fit specialist Janus Electric announced a definitive supply agreement with Canadian lithium‑ion specialist Electrovaya to power its upcoming Class 8 battery‑swap semi‑truck platform. Under the deal, Electrovaya will deliver up to 400 kWh modular battery packs, each built from its proprietary lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) chemistry, optimized for rapid swapping in under five minutes. The packs are designed with a standardized “swap‑ready” interface that aligns with Janus’s patented swap‑station, allowing a fully depleted truck to be exchanged for a fully charged unit in a single pit‑stop.

The partnership addresses a key bottleneck in North‑American heavy‑duty electrification: the lack of a reliable, high‑energy‑density source that can be replenished quickly without long dwell times. Janus projects an initial rollout of 250 trucks in 2027, scaling to 1,000 units by 2028, with major logistics customers already placing conditional orders. Electrovaya will also co‑locate a 2 GWh battery‑pack manufacturing line in the United States to meet the anticipated demand, creating roughly 300 new jobs.

Industry analysts see the deal as a litmus test for the viability of battery‑swap models in the long‑haul market, which has traditionally favored fast‑charging solutions. The 400 kWh capacity translates to roughly 800 km (500 mi) of range under typical freight loads, comparable to diesel‑fuel ranges and sufficient for most regional routes. If the swap infrastructure scales as planned, the model could shave up to 30 % off total operating costs versus conventional diesel, primarily by eliminating idle charging time and reducing battery degradation through uniform charge cycles.

The agreement also underscores a broader shift toward LFP chemistry for heavy‑duty applications, driven by its thermal stability, lower cost, and reduced reliance on cobalt. Electrovaya’s involvement brings a proven cell platform that has already passed automotive safety standards, potentially accelerating regulatory approvals for the swap system.

Overall, the Janus‑Electrovaya partnership could reshape the heavy‑duty electrification playbook, offering a pragmatic alternative to fast‑charging networks while leveraging domestic manufacturing to mitigate supply‑chain risks. ## 02 Volkswagen Expands EV Battery Recall to Over 85,000 Units, Adding Audi Q4 Models
Volkswagen Group disclosed that its ongoing EV battery recall now encompasses more than 85,000 vehicles worldwide, extending the scope to include Audi’s Q4 e‑tron and Q4 Sportback models alongside the previously affected Volkswagen ID.4 fleet. The recall stems from a cooling‑system defect in the high‑voltage battery pack that can lead to localized overheating under high‑state‑of‑charge conditions, especially in hot climates.

Affected units span model years 2022‑2025 and carry battery capacities ranging from 52 kWh to 77 kWh. Volkswagen’s technical service bulletin mandates a software update that limits maximum charge to 80 % of nominal capacity, thereby reducing thermal stress while preserving usable range. Early field data suggest a typical range reduction of 10‑15 km (6‑9 mi), a trade‑off deemed acceptable given the safety benefit.

The recall also triggers a hardware inspection of the battery’s thermal‑management module, with replacement of the faulty coolant pump in approximately 12 % of the fleet. Volkswagen estimates the total cost of the recall at €250 million, a figure that includes logistics, parts, and labor. The company has set up a dedicated hotline and will prioritize service appointments in regions with the highest ambient temperatures, such as Southern Europe, the United States Sun Belt, and parts of Asia.

From a market perspective, the recall highlights the growing pains of rapid EV adoption and the importance of robust battery‑management systems. Analysts note that the 80 % charge‑limit is a common mitigation strategy, already employed by other OEMs facing similar thermal‑runaway concerns. The move may also accelerate the industry’s shift toward more resilient chemistries, such as LFP, which inherently tolerate higher states of charge without significant temperature rise.

Regulators in the EU and the U.S. have praised Volkswagen’s proactive communication but urged tighter pre‑production testing of battery‑thermal designs. The recall could influence upcoming EU Battery‑Passport regulations, which will require detailed reporting of battery safety incidents. Volkswagen has pledged to integrate the lessons learned into its next‑generation MEB platform, slated for launch in 2028, with an emphasis on improved coolant flow paths and advanced cell‑level temperature monitoring.

Overall, the expanded recall underscores the delicate balance between maximizing range and ensuring safety, a challenge that will shape battery‑pack engineering for the next decade. [Electrek](https://electrek.co/2026/10/28/volkswagens-ev-battery-recall-reaches-over-85000-with-audi/)  

## 03 Tesla Powerwall Software Update Sparks Debate Over Home‑Battery Ownership
Tesla rolled out a firmware update to its Powerwall home‑energy storage system that has ignited a heated discussion about user control and third‑party integration. In Australia, the update disables the ability of external inverters—such as those from Sonnen or Enphase—to draw power from the Powerwall, effectively locking the battery to Tesla’s own ecosystem. The change follows a series of complaints from Australian owners who used the Powerwall as a “grid‑support” battery for solar‑plus‑storage setups, allowing surplus solar to be dispatched through third‑party inverters.

The Powerwall 2 and the newer Powerwall 3, both rated at 13.5 kWh usable capacity with a 5 kW continuous discharge rate, now enforce a “Tesla‑only” mode unless the owner opts into the new PowerShare feature, which is currently available only in the United States. PowerShare enables a Powerwall to supply power to a second home or a neighbor’s house via the Tesla app, effectively turning the battery into a micro‑grid node. The feature is marketed as a way to monetize stored energy, but it also raises concerns about data privacy and grid‑impact modeling.

Tesla’s rationale, as outlined in the update notes, is to protect battery health and ensure compliance with local grid codes that vary widely across jurisdictions. However, consumer advocacy groups argue that the move undermines the “energy‑as‑a‑service” model that many homeowners adopted when they purchased the Powerwall, especially in markets where third‑party inverter integration was a key selling point.

The update also introduces a new “Smart Reserve” algorithm that automatically reserves 20 % of stored energy for emergency backup, a feature that aligns with recent regulatory pushes for resilience in residential energy storage. Early field tests suggest the algorithm can improve backup duration by up to 30 % without sacrificing daily cycling efficiency.

Industry observers see Tesla’s actions as a bellwether for how major battery manufacturers will navigate the tension between open‑system flexibility and proprietary control. As more utilities explore demand‑response programs that rely on residential storage, the ability—or inability—to interoperate with third‑party hardware could shape market adoption rates. The move may also prompt legislative scrutiny; the Australian Energy Regulator has indicated it will review the update for potential anti‑competitive implications.

Overall, the Powerwall firmware change highlights the evolving governance of distributed energy resources, where software can redefine hardware capabilities overnight. Whether Tesla’s approach will spur broader industry standardization or fragment the residential storage market remains to be seen.
