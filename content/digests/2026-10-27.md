## 01 BYD Unveils Ultra‑Luxury Sedan with First‑Generation Solid‑State Cells
Chinese automaker BYD announced that its next flagship electric sedan, tentatively named the **Denza Z9 GT II**, will be the company’s first vehicle to employ solid‑state battery technology. The car is slated to debut at a high‑profile event next week and will feature Rolls‑Royce‑inspired “coach doors,” a 100 kWh solid‑state pack, and an 800 km WLTP range—roughly 15 % longer than BYD’s current best‑in‑class LFP models.

The solid‑state cells are supplied by **Sila Nanotechnologies**, marking BYD’s first partnership with a non‑Chinese solid‑state developer. According to BYD, the new cells operate at a nominal voltage of 4.2 V per cell and achieve an energy density of **420 Wh kg⁻¹**, a jump of about 30 % over the company’s latest LFP chemistry. The pack architecture uses a modular “stack‑and‑swap” design that allows individual cell modules to be replaced in under two minutes, a feature that could revive interest in battery‑swap services.

Why it matters: Solid‑state batteries have long been touted for their safety (no liquid electrolyte) and higher energy density, but commercial roll‑outs have been limited to pilot programs. BYD’s move signals that a major Chinese OEM believes the technology is ready for mass production, potentially accelerating the global shift away from conventional lithium‑ion chemistries. The partnership with Sila also gives BYD a foothold in the emerging solid‑state supply chain, which could reshape raw‑material demand for cobalt and nickel.

The announcement arrives as the industry grapples with supply‑chain constraints on lithium and nickel, and as governments push for higher range standards. If BYD can deliver on its performance claims, the Z9 GT II could set a new benchmark for premium EVs and force competitors to accelerate their own solid‑state programs.

## 02 Huawei’s Newcool Unit Joins CATL’s 2,520‑Station Battery‑Swap Network
Huawei’s automotive subsidiary, **Newcool**, signed a strategic cooperation framework with **CATL** and its battery‑swap arm **CAES** on 19 October. The deal integrates Newcool‑branded electric vehicles into CATL’s **“Choco‑Swap”** network, which already operates **2,520 stations** across 31 Chinese provinces and major metropolitan areas.

Under the agreement, Newcool will adopt CATL’s standardized swap‑interface hardware and software, enabling its upcoming EV models to complete a full‑battery exchange in **under three minutes**. The network’s backend uses a cloud‑based energy‑management platform that balances grid load by dispatching swapped‑out batteries to stationary storage sites during off‑peak hours.

The partnership is significant for several reasons. First, it gives Huawei—a company traditionally known for telecommunications—a direct role in the EV ecosystem, leveraging its expertise in 5G connectivity to provide real‑time diagnostics and predictive maintenance for swap stations. Second, the scale of CATL’s network provides Newcool with immediate nationwide coverage, sidestepping the chicken‑and‑egg problem that has hampered earlier swap ventures. Finally, the collaboration includes a joint R&D fund of **US$150 million** aimed at developing next‑generation **solid‑state swap‑ready cells**, potentially lowering the weight and cost of swap‑compatible packs.

Analysts see the move as a test of whether battery swapping can regain relevance in a market increasingly dominated by fast‑charging infrastructure. If Newcool’s models achieve strong sales, the combined hardware‑software ecosystem could prove that swapping remains a viable solution for high‑utilisation fleets such as ride‑hailing and logistics.

## 03 Tesla Designs the Cybercab Around Low‑Cost LFP Battery Chemistry
Tesla’s autonomous‑taxi concept, the **Cybercab**, has been revealed in a design brief that emphasizes a **lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP)** battery pack as the baseline chemistry. The vehicle is expected to launch in 2028 as part of Tesla’s “Robotaxi” fleet, and the design team chose LFP to keep the unit cost below **US$15,000** for the battery pack, roughly **30 % cheaper** than the nickel‑cobalt‑aluminum (NCA) packs used in current Model Y and Model 3 vehicles.

The proposed pack size is **350 kWh**, delivering a **350 km** (WLTP) range—sufficient for dense‑city operation where daily mileage rarely exceeds 200 km. The LFP cells are arranged in a **high‑voltage 400 V** architecture, allowing the use of existing Supercharger infrastructure while simplifying thermal management. Because LFP chemistry tolerates higher charge rates, the Cybercab can accept a **250 kW** fast charge, reaching 80 % state‑of‑charge in **15 minutes**.

Tesla’s decision reflects a broader industry trend toward LFP for cost‑sensitive, high‑cycle‑life applications. LFP’s longer cycle life (up to 4,000 cycles) and reduced reliance on cobalt align with Tesla’s sustainability goals and its plan to recycle **over 90 %** of battery material in‑house by 2030. Moreover, the use of a single chemistry across both consumer EVs and robotaxis could streamline supply‑chain logistics, reducing the need for multiple cell factories.

Critics note that the 350 km range may limit the Cybercab’s suitability for suburban or intercity services, but Tesla argues that the vehicle’s autonomous routing software will optimise charging stops, and the lower cost per kilowatt‑hour will make the overall fleet economics more attractive to ride‑hailing operators.
