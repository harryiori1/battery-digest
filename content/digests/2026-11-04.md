## 01 CATL, Octopus Energy and Ampace Team Up to Bring Modular Home Batteries to Europe

A three‑way partnership announced on 4 November brings together China’s battery giant CATL, UK‑based clean‑energy retailer Octopus Energy, and German storage specialist Ampace. The consortium will develop and market modular home‑energy storage systems built around CATL’s latest lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) pouch cells.  

The first pilot will launch in the United Kingdom in early 2027, with a parallel rollout in Germany slated for 2028. Each unit will be a stackable 5 kWh module that can be combined up to 20 kWh for whole‑home use, delivering a round‑trip efficiency of 96 % and a warranty of ten years. CATL will supply the cells from its new 30 GWh LFP line in Ningde, while Ampace will handle system integration and certification. Octopus Energy will market the product through its “Octopus Power” subscription, bundling the battery with its time‑of‑use tariffs to smooth residential demand peaks.

The partners aim to produce roughly 1 GWh of battery packs by 2028, enough to power about 200,000 UK homes. By leveraging CATL’s economies of scale, the price point is projected at £1,200 per 5 kWh module—significantly lower than current market offerings. The collaboration also includes a recycling clause: end‑of‑life modules will be returned to CATL’s Shanghai facility for material recovery, aligning with Europe’s upcoming battery‑passport regulations.

Analysts see the deal as a strategic move to lock in market share before the EU’s 2029 mandatory battery‑passport rollout, which will require full traceability of cell chemistry, origin, and second‑life usage. If successful, the partnership could set a template for other OEM‑retailer alliances across the continent.

## 02 EU Battery‑Passport Rules Set to Reshape the U.S. E‑Bike Market

The European Union is preparing to enforce a “battery passport” regime beginning February 2027, obligating every new e‑bike battery sold in the bloc to carry a digital identity. The passport will record the battery’s chemistry, raw‑material provenance, manufacturing date, performance metrics, and end‑of‑life recycling pathway.  

For U.S. manufacturers, the rule presents both a compliance challenge and a market opportunity. Companies that already embed traceability into their supply chains—such as Bosch and Panasonic—will face fewer retrofitting costs, while smaller players may need to invest in new data‑logging hardware and cloud services. The regulation also mandates that the passport be accessible to consumers via a QR code, enabling owners to verify sustainability claims and to locate certified recyclers.

Industry groups estimate the added cost per battery will be between €2–€5, a modest increase relative to the typical €300–€600 price tag of a mid‑range e‑bike pack. However, the requirement could accelerate the adoption of standardized cell formats, as manufacturers seek to simplify data collection across multiple models.  

U.S. analysts predict a ripple effect: many global e‑bike brands will adopt the passport globally to avoid maintaining separate product lines, effectively raising the baseline of transparency for all markets. The move also dovetails with the EU’s broader circular‑economy agenda, which aims to recycle at least 70 % of battery materials by 2030.  

The policy could spur growth in second‑life applications, as documented‑rich batteries become more attractive for stationary storage projects. Companies already experimenting with repurposing e‑bike packs—such as the startup highlighted in the third story below—stand to benefit from a clearer regulatory framework.

## 03 Startup ReVolt Energy Turns Used E‑Bike Cells into Low‑Cost Stationary Storage

InsideEVs reported on 4 November that a Berlin‑based startup, ReVolt Energy, has begun commercializing second‑life battery modules sourced from discarded e‑bike and e‑scooter packs. The company’s “ReCell” system reconditions up to 150 Wh pouch cells, testing each for capacity loss, internal resistance, and safety compliance. Viable cells are then assembled into 10 kWh, 48 V modules that can be stacked for larger installations.  

ReVolt’s pricing model undercuts conventional lithium‑ion storage, offering the modules at $190 per kWh—about 30 % cheaper than new LFP packs. The firm targets off‑grid cabins, small‑scale commercial backup, and micro‑grid projects in emerging markets where cost is a primary barrier. Early pilots in the Netherlands and Kenya have demonstrated 95 % round‑trip efficiency and a projected 5‑year lifespan, well within the 7‑year warranty offered by ReVolt.  

Funding for the venture includes a €5 million seed round led by the European Battery Alliance, with additional support from the German Ministry for Economic Affairs, which sees the project as a way to meet the EU’s 2030 recycling targets. ReVolt also partners with local e‑bike rental operators to collect end‑of‑life packs, creating a closed‑loop supply chain that reduces transportation emissions.  

The startup’s approach addresses two pain points in the battery ecosystem: the growing volume of small‑format lithium cells reaching end‑of‑life, and the scarcity of affordable storage for distributed energy resources. By giving these cells a second life, ReVolt not only extracts residual value but also eases pressure on raw‑material demand, particularly for cobalt and nickel, which are scarce in the e‑bike cell mix.

## 04 Bluetti Elite 300 Portable Power Station Slashed to $1,099 – 3,014 Wh Capacity

Electrek highlighted a limited‑time discount on Bluetti’s Elite 300 portable power station, now priced at $1,099, a $600 reduction from its launch price. The unit packs a 3,014 Wh lithium‑ion battery, supports 240 V AC output up to 1,800 W, and includes multiple USB‑C and DC ports for versatile charging. Bluetti claims a 95 % round‑trip efficiency and a built‑in battery‑management system that limits over‑charge and deep‑discharge.  

The price cut is part of Bluetti’s “Halloween Deals” campaign aimed at boosting adoption of home‑backup and off‑grid solutions ahead of the winter season. Analysts note that the Elite 300’s price now competes with entry‑level Tesla Powerwall units on a per‑kWh basis, potentially expanding the market for portable, modular storage.

## 05 Rio Tinto Tests ‘Hot‑Brick’ Thermal Battery to Replace Fossil Fuel Steam at Alumina Plant

Rio Tinto announced a pilot of a “hot‑brick” thermal battery at its Yarwun alumina refinery in Queensland. The system stores excess midday solar electricity as heat in high‑temperature ceramic bricks, which can later generate steam for the refinery’s process heat needs after sunset. The pilot aims to store up to 200 MWh of thermal energy, reducing the plant’s reliance on natural‑gas‑fired boilers by an estimated 30 % during peak demand periods.  

While not an electrochemical battery, the technology qualifies as an energy‑storage system and reflects a broader industry shift toward decarbonizing heavy‑industry processes. If successful, the hot‑brick concept could be replicated at other high‑temperature industrial sites, offering a low‑cost alternative to conventional batteries for bulk heat storage.
