## 01 QuantumScape Unveils Osprey All‑Solid‑State Cell with 1,000‑Cycle Life
QuantumScape announced the commercial‑grade Osprey platform, its first all‑solid‑state lithium‑sulphide cell that can endure 1,000 full charge‑discharge cycles to 80 % capacity while operating at a modest one‑atmosphere pressure. The breakthrough replaces the liquid electrolyte in conventional lithium‑ion cathodes with a sulphide‑based solid electrolyte, eliminating the need for heavy clamping systems that have plagued competing solid‑state designs. In laboratory tests, Osprey cells delivered an energy density of 350 Wh kg⁻¹ and a power rating of 1.5 kW kg⁻¹, enough to power a midsize electric sedan for over 500 km on a single charge.  

The company claims the cells can be produced using existing roll‑to‑roll manufacturing lines with only minor equipment upgrades, a claim that could dramatically shorten the time to volume production. QuantumScape’s roadmap now targets pilot‑scale production in 2028, followed by a 2029 start of volume manufacturing in partnership with a major OEM (still undisclosed). If the performance and cost targets hold, Osprey could challenge the dominance of liquid‑electrolyte lithium‑ion cells in both passenger‑vehicle and stationary‑storage markets.  

Industry analysts note that the 1,000‑cycle figure at ambient pressure addresses two long‑standing hurdles: cycle life and manufacturing complexity. “Most solid‑state labs report impressive energy density but at the cost of high pressure or low cycle life,” said a senior analyst at BloombergNEF. “QuantumScape’s claim, if verified, would be a game‑changer for the entire battery supply chain.”  

The announcement arrives as the U.S. and EU are tightening subsidies for next‑generation batteries, making a commercially viable solid‑state cell especially timely. QuantumScape’s next steps include third‑party validation of the cycle‑life data and scaling the sulphide electrolyte production to metric‑ton levels.

## 02 China’s October Power‑Battery Installations Reach Record 99.4 GWh, LFP Share 86.9 %
China’s National Energy Administration released data showing that October 2026 saw 99.4 GWh of new power‑battery capacity installed, the highest monthly total on record for stationary storage. The surge pushed the cumulative 2026 installations to 1.2 TWh, a 23 % increase over the same period last year. Notably, lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) chemistry accounted for 86.9 % of the new capacity, up from 82 % in September, reflecting the country’s continued preference for cobalt‑free chemistries in grid‑scale applications.  

The installations were dominated by utility‑scale projects in the North China and Yangtze River basins, where renewable‑energy curtailment remains a challenge. The largest single project, a 12 GWh LFP battery farm in Inner Mongolia, is slated to provide frequency regulation and peak‑shaving services for the regional grid.  

Analysts attribute the record month to a combination of policy incentives—such as the “Renewable Energy Storage Subsidy” that offers up to 30 % cost support for LFP projects—and falling battery‑pack prices, which have dropped to an average of $85 kWh for utility‑scale LFP systems, a 12 % year‑over‑year decline. The data also show a modest rise in sodium‑ion installations, which now represent 2.3 % of the total, hinting at early diversification beyond lithium.  

The implications for the global supply chain are significant. China’s dominance in LFP cathode material production—accounting for roughly 70 % of worldwide output—means that continued domestic demand could tighten export availability, potentially raising prices for overseas developers. Conversely, the scale‑up may accelerate economies of scale, driving down costs for LFP cells used in electric‑bus and low‑speed EV segments worldwide.  

The report underscores China’s strategic shift toward safer, longer‑life, and lower‑cost storage solutions as part of its broader carbon‑neutrality goals, with LFP now the de‑facto standard for large‑scale energy storage.

## 03 EU’s Industrial Accelerator Act Faces Critical Vote, Could Shape Europe’s Battery Future
The European Parliament is set to vote next week on the Industrial Accelerator Act (IAA), a sweeping legislative package intended to fast‑track the continent’s battery value chain. The bill proposes €30 billion of public funding, tax incentives, and streamlined permitting for battery cell factories, raw‑material processing, and recycling facilities, with the explicit goal of achieving a 50 % domestic share of EU‑produced battery cells by 2035.  

Proponents argue the IAA is essential to reduce Europe’s reliance on Asian supply chains, especially for cathode materials such as nickel and cobalt, which currently see 80 % of their processing done outside the bloc. The act also mandates a “battery passport” system, requiring every cell sold in the EU to carry traceability data on material origin, carbon footprint, and end‑of‑life recycling pathways.  

However, industry groups warn that the funding targets may be insufficient to compete with the massive subsidies offered by China and the United States. The European Battery Alliance, representing major OEMs and cell manufacturers, has called for an additional €10 billion to bridge the gap, citing the risk that the IAA could become a “paper policy” without real impact on capacity build‑out.  

Environmental NGOs have raised concerns about the act’s emphasis on scaling production without equally strong provisions for sustainable mining and circularity. While the legislation includes a recycling quota of 70 % by weight for end‑of‑life batteries, critics argue that the enforcement mechanisms are vague.  

If passed, the IAA could unlock up to 200 GWh of new cell capacity in Europe by 2030, according to a joint study by the European Commission and the International Energy Agency. The study projects that such capacity would create roughly 150,000 jobs across the supply chain and cut EU battery import dependence from 85 % to 45 % within five years.  

The upcoming vote is therefore a litmus test for Europe’s ability to coordinate policy, finance, and industry to secure a competitive battery ecosystem. A narrow defeat could stall the continent’s ambitions, while a decisive approval may position Europe as a credible alternative to the Asian battery dominance.
