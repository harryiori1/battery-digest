## 01 VW and Skoda Deploy OTA Battery Pre‑conditioning Update  
Volkswagen Group announced that an over‑the‑air (OTA) software update will add active battery pre‑conditioning to older models of its ID.4, ID.5 and Skoda Enyaq families. The update, which began rolling out on 3 November 2026, enables the vehicle’s thermal‑management system to warm the lithium‑ion pack to its optimal operating temperature before the driver initiates a trip.  

Pre‑conditioning has been a standard feature on newer EVs, but many owners of early‑generation models complained about rapid range loss in cold climates. The new code works in conjunction with the manufacturers’ mobile apps, allowing users to schedule a warm‑up window (typically 15‑30 minutes) or trigger it automatically when the cabin heater is turned on. Early field data from VW’s European test fleet shows a 5‑8 % recovery of nominal range at –10 °C, translating to roughly 15‑20 km of extra driving per charge.  

From a business perspective, the move addresses a persistent pain point that has been cited in customer satisfaction surveys and could reduce warranty‑related battery‑temperature claims. It also demonstrates how software can extend the functional lifespan of existing hardware, a trend that rivals such as Tesla and BYD have been exploiting for years.  

The rollout will first reach markets in the EU and the UK, with a planned expansion to North America in early 2027 pending regulatory clearance. The update does not require any hardware changes, keeping costs low for both OEM and owners.  

Industry analysts see the pre‑conditioning patch as a low‑cost way to improve cold‑weather performance without redesigning the pack, reinforcing the idea that “software is the new battery”. It may also set a precedent for other legacy EVs to receive similar upgrades, potentially reshaping the aftermarket service model for EV batteries.

## 02 Tesla Powerwall Software Changes Spark Debate Over Home‑Battery Control  
Tesla’s latest Powerwall firmware update, released on 2 November 2026, has ignited a heated discussion among residential energy users and regulators. In Australia, the new version (v12.4) disables the previously supported third‑party inverter integration that allowed owners to pair a Powerwall with non‑Tesla solar inverters. The change effectively locks the battery to Tesla’s own inverter ecosystem, a move Tesla says is intended to “enhance safety and grid compliance”.  

Australian consumer groups argue the update undermines the principle of hardware‑agnostic energy storage, potentially forcing owners to replace costly inverters or abandon existing solar setups. The Australian Energy Regulator has opened a formal inquiry, citing concerns about market competition and consumer rights.  

Conversely, the same update introduces “PowerShare” in North America, a feature that lets a Powerwall owner allocate excess stored energy to a neighboring home via a secure, blockchain‑based transaction protocol. PowerShare aims to smooth peak‑load demand and could become a building‑block for community micro‑grids. Early pilot data from a California subdivision shows a 12 % reduction in peak‑grid draw during hot afternoons when PowerShare is active.  

The dual nature of the update—restrictive in one market, expansive in another—highlights Tesla’s region‑specific strategy for its energy‑storage business. Analysts note that while the Australian restriction may alienate a segment of the market, the PowerShare rollout could open new revenue streams and strengthen Tesla’s position as a grid‑services provider.  

Regulators in both regions are watching closely. In Australia, the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) is assessing whether the firmware change breaches competition law. In the United States, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) is evaluating the interoperability standards of PowerShare under its recent “distributed energy resources” framework.  

The episode underscores a broader industry tension: the balance between proprietary technology that can ensure safety and performance, and the open‑access model that many argue is essential for a truly decentralized energy future.

## 03 Chinese Bus Battery Resale Hits Premium Over 119% for 125 Retired Packs  
A recent report from the Chinese industry portal 第一电动 (Diyi Dian Dong) reveals that 125 retired lithium‑ion battery packs harvested from decommissioned city buses were sold for a total of 3 million yuan (≈ US $420,000) on a secondary‑market platform. The transaction, completed on 5 November 2026, represents a premium of more than 119 % over the original purchase price of the packs when they were first installed in 2018‑2020.  

Each pack, originally rated at roughly 100 kWh, has been repurposed for stationary energy‑storage applications such as micro‑grids, backup power for telecom sites, and commercial load‑shifting. The high resale price reflects a growing demand for “second‑life” batteries in China’s rapidly expanding renewable‑energy sector, where the cost of new cells remains elevated due to raw‑material price volatility.  

The report notes that the seller performed a full health assessment using a combination of capacity‑fade testing, impedance spectroscopy, and thermal imaging before listing the packs. Certified capacity retention averaged 78 % of the original 100 kWh, translating to usable capacities of 78 kWh per pack. The platform also offers a warranty of 12 months on performance, a service that has helped build buyer confidence.  

Industry observers see this as a sign that the Chinese second‑life market is maturing. The Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) recently released guidelines encouraging the standardized testing and certification of used EV batteries, aiming to create a transparent pricing mechanism. Analysts estimate that by 2028, China could host over 30 GWh of second‑life storage capacity, enough to support roughly 5 % of the nation’s projected renewable‑energy curtailment.  

The premium achieved in this transaction also signals that investors view repurposed bus batteries as a relatively low‑risk asset compared with new cell production, which is subject to supply‑chain bottlenecks for nickel, cobalt and lithium. Moreover, the environmental benefit—avoiding the need for fresh raw‑material extraction and reducing waste—adds a sustainability premium that aligns with China’s “dual‑carbon” goals.  

The sale underscores the economic viability of extracting value from end‑of‑life EV batteries and may encourage more fleet operators to establish formal battery‑return programs, further fueling the nascent recycling and reuse ecosystem.
