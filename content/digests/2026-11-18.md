## 01 Ford’s BlueOval Battery Park Begins LFP Cell Production for Fathom Pickup
Ford announced that its newly built BlueOval Battery Park in Michigan has started shipping production‑grade lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) cells to power the prototype Fathom pickup, a $30,000 electric truck aimed at the commercial‑light‑duty market. The plant, a joint venture between Ford and SK On, began low‑volume production in early 2026 and is now delivering its first batch of 70 Ah pouch cells to Ford’s engineering center in Kentucky for integration into the Fathom’s 70 kWh battery pack.

The move is significant for several reasons. First, it marks Ford’s transition from a pure vehicle assembler to an integrated battery manufacturer, giving the automaker greater control over cost, supply chain resilience, and cell chemistry selection. LFP chemistry, while offering lower energy density than nickel‑cobalt‑manganese (NCM) cells, provides superior thermal stability, longer cycle life (up to 3,000 cycles), and eliminates the need for cobalt—a material under increasing geopolitical and ethical scrutiny.

Second, the Fathom platform is designed around a modular battery architecture that can be scaled from 50 kWh to 100 kWh, allowing Ford to target a range of commercial applications from last‑mile delivery to utility‑grade work trucks. Early testing indicates a real‑world range of roughly 250 miles on a single charge, comfortably meeting the needs of most urban fleets.

Finally, the production rollout aligns with broader industry trends toward domestic cell manufacturing in the United States. By localizing cell supply, Ford hopes to mitigate the bottlenecks that have plagued the EV market since 2023, when global lithium‑ion demand outstripped capacity and led to price spikes. The BlueOval facility is slated to reach a 30 GWh annual capacity by 2028, positioning Ford to supply not only its own models but potentially other OEMs seeking LFP cells.

Overall, Ford’s LFP launch underscores a strategic shift toward cost‑effective, cobalt‑free batteries for mass‑market EVs, while also reinforcing the company’s commitment to a vertically integrated supply chain. ## 02 U.S. Energy‑Storage Installations Top 200 GWh Amid Rapid Growth
The U.S. energy‑storage market crossed the 200 GWh cumulative installation milestone in Q3 2026, according to a CleanTechnica report that aggregates data from the U.S. Energy Information Administration and industry trackers. The third quarter alone added 14.3 GWh of new capacity, driven primarily by utility‑scale lithium‑ion projects and a surge in behind‑the‑meter (BTM) storage for commercial and industrial customers.

Utility‑scale deployments now account for roughly 65 % of total installed capacity, with the largest projects—such as the 500 MWh “Sunrise” battery in Texas and the 400 MWh “Midwest Grid‑Stabilizer” in Illinois—leveraging long‑duration lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) chemistries to provide frequency regulation, peak‑shaving, and renewable‑energy firming services. The report notes that the average cost of utility‑scale storage has fallen to $115/kWh, a 22 % decline from the same period in 2025, reflecting economies of scale, improved cell energy density, and the maturation of supply‑chain financing.

On the BTM side, corporate and data‑center customers are increasingly adopting hybrid solar‑plus‑storage solutions to hedge against grid volatility and to meet ESG commitments. The average BTM system size grew from 1.2 MWh in 2024 to 1.8 MWh in 2026, with many projects now integrating advanced energy‑management software that can dispatch stored power in sub‑second intervals.

Policy incentives continue to play a pivotal role. The Inflation Reduction Act’s tax credit for storage (up to 30 % of project cost) remains fully refundable, and several states—California, New York, and Illinois—have introduced additional rebates for long‑duration storage that can sustain discharge for eight hours or more.

The 200 GWh milestone signals that storage is moving from a niche ancillary service to a core component of the U.S. grid architecture, enabling higher penetrations of wind and solar while providing resilience against extreme weather events. Analysts project that cumulative U.S. storage could reach 350 GWh by the end of 2027 if current deployment rates hold.
