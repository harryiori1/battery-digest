## 01 Briggs & Stratton Launches 16 kWh LFP Backup Battery for Residential Generators
Briggs & Stratton, a name long associated with gasoline‑powered standby generators, is stepping into the electric‑age with a 16 kWh lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) battery pack designed for residential backup applications. The new unit, announced on 22 November, is built on a modular architecture that allows owners to stack up to three modules for a total of 48 kWh of usable energy, enough to power an average U.S. home for two to three days during a prolonged outage.

The pack uses LFP chemistry, prized for its thermal stability, long cycle life (over 4,000 full cycles) and tolerance to deep discharge—attributes that align with the rugged reliability expectations of generator users. Each module integrates a built‑in battery management system (BMS) that communicates with Briggs & Stratton’s existing generator control units, automatically switching the load between the generator and the battery to maximize fuel savings. In practice, the system can run a typical 5 kW home load for roughly three hours on a single module, extending to nine hours when three modules are combined.

Pricing has not been disclosed, but Briggs & Stratton positions the solution as a “drop‑in” upgrade for its existing generator lineup, targeting homeowners who want to reduce diesel consumption while still having a reliable backup source. The company also promises a 10‑year warranty on the battery cells, matching the lifespan of its generators.

The move reflects a broader trend of traditional power‑equipment manufacturers adding stationary storage to their portfolios, a shift accelerated by the increasing frequency of extreme weather events and the growing consumer appetite for clean, silent backup power. By leveraging LFP’s safety profile and cost‑effective scaling, Briggs & Stratton aims to capture a niche that bridges the gap between conventional generators and full‑home battery systems like Tesla’s Powerwall.

## 02 Huawei Unveils 20 kW AI‑Managed Home Battery to Compete with Tesla Powerwall
Huawei, the Chinese telecom and consumer‑electronics giant, announced a 20 kW home battery system on 14 November that directly challenges Tesla’s Powerwall in both power output and intelligence. The system combines silicon‑carbide (SiC) inverter technology with Huawei’s proprietary AI‑driven energy management platform, which continuously optimizes charge‑discharge cycles based on real‑time grid pricing, weather forecasts, and household consumption patterns.

While Huawei has not released the exact energy capacity, analysts estimate a usable storage of roughly 30 kWh, based on the 20 kW continuous discharge rating and typical depth‑of‑discharge limits for residential systems. The battery is modular, allowing users to add or remove units in 10 kWh increments, and it can be installed either as a wall‑mounted unit or a floor‑standing rack, depending on space constraints.

Key differentiators include:
* **SiC Inverters:** Offer up to 15 % higher efficiency than conventional silicon inverters, reducing conversion losses during both charging and discharging.
* **AI Energy Management:** Uses Huawei’s cloud‑based AI to predict household load and schedule battery usage for peak‑shaving, demand‑response, and backup scenarios, promising up to 12 % lower electricity bills in pilot studies.
* **Scalable Architecture:** Up to 100 kW of combined power can be achieved by linking multiple units, making the system suitable for larger homes or small commercial sites.

Huawei’s entry into the residential storage market underscores the company’s broader strategy to diversify beyond smartphones and telecom equipment, leveraging its expertise in power electronics and AI. If the system can deliver on its performance promises, it could pressure incumbent players to accelerate their own AI‑driven features and push the industry toward higher‑power, more intelligent home storage solutions.

## 03 Ford’s First LFP Cells Ship to Kentucky for Fathom Pickup Prototypes
Ford Motor Co. confirmed on 18 November that the first batch of lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) cells produced at its newly built BlueOval Battery Park in Michigan has left the factory and is en route to the company’s Kentucky assembly plant. The cells will be integrated into the upcoming Fathom electric pickup, a prototype slated for limited‑run testing later this year.

The production run consists of approximately 1.2 GWh of LFP cells, enough to assemble roughly 10,000 Fathom battery packs. Each pack is expected to deliver a usable capacity of 70 kWh, providing an estimated 300 mile (480 km) range under the EPA cycle—sufficient for most work‑truck duties while keeping costs below $100 kWh, a price point Ford has been targeting for mass‑market EVs.

Ford’s LFP chemistry is supplied under a licensing agreement with CATL, the Chinese battery giant, but the cells are manufactured entirely in‑house, marking a significant step toward vertical integration. The move is intended to reduce reliance on external suppliers, improve supply‑chain resilience, and give Ford greater control over cell quality and cost.

Industry analysts view the shipment as a validation of Ford’s “battery‑first” strategy, which aims to produce at least 200 GWh of battery capacity by 2030 across its global plants. By using LFP—known for its lower raw‑material cost, superior safety, and longer cycle life—Ford hopes to keep the Fathom’s price competitive against internal‑combustion rivals while sidestepping the cobalt‑supply constraints that have plagued many EV manufacturers.

The arrival of the cells in Kentucky also signals that Ford’s next‑generation EVs, including the upcoming F‑150 Lightning refresh, could soon benefit from in‑house LFP production, potentially accelerating the rollout of lower‑cost electric trucks across the U.S. market.
