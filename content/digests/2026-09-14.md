## 01 BYD Says It Leads the Solid‑State Battery Race, Plans First EV for 2027  

Chinese automaker BYD announced that it now occupies “the leading position” in the global solid‑state battery (SSB) race, according to Executive Vice‑President Stella Li in an interview published on 14 September 2026 [Electrek](https://electrek.co/2026/09/14/byd-leading-solid-state-battery-race-first-ev-2027/). BYD says its in‑house R&D team has already produced laboratory‑scale solid‑state cells that achieve an energy density of 530 Wh kg⁻¹, surpassing the 250‑300 Wh kg⁻¹ typical of today’s liquid‑electrolyte lithium‑ion packs.  

The company plans to start pilot‑line production in early 2026, with a target annual output of 30 GWh by 2028. The first vehicle slated to carry the new cells is a compact crossover slated for a 2027 market launch, positioned as a premium alternative to BYD’s current “Blade” battery models. The solid‑state pack will use a sulfide‑based electrolyte, promising a 30 % reduction in cell‑level internal resistance and the ability to operate safely at temperatures up to 150 °C, eliminating the need for active cooling.  

Why it matters: If BYD can scale SSBs at volume, the technology could dramatically shrink battery pack size, lower vehicle weight, and improve safety—key levers for extending EV range and reducing total‑cost‑of‑ownership. BYD’s claim also intensifies competition with Japanese and Korean rivals such as Toyota, Samsung SDI, and SK On, all of which have announced multi‑year SSB roadmaps.  

The announcement comes at a time when global lithium‑ion demand is projected to exceed 2 TWh in 2027, and supply‑chain constraints on nickel and cobalt are prompting manufacturers to explore chemistries that rely less on these metals. Solid‑state cells, which can be built with high‑voltage nickel‑rich cathodes and lithium‑metal anodes, could alleviate some of those pressures.  

If BYD’s timeline holds, the 2027 rollout would be the first mass‑produced SSB‑powered EV, giving the company a first‑mover advantage in a market that is still largely speculative. Industry analysts will be watching the pilot‑line output data closely, as any deviation from the announced schedule could reshape the competitive landscape for the next decade.

## 02 UK Announces First Fleet of Battery‑Electric Trains to Replace Diesel Units  

The United Kingdom’s Department for Transport (DfT) unveiled a £1.2 billion programme on 12 September 2026 to procure a fleet of 120 battery‑electric multiple units (BEMUs) for regional services across northern England [Electrek](https://electrek.co/2026/09/12/new-fleet-of-battery-electric-trains-promises-faster-more-reliable-uk-travel/). The trains, built by a joint venture between Hitachi Rail and Alstom, will be equipped with modular lithium‑ion battery packs ranging from 1.0 to 1.5 MWh, delivering a maximum range of 200 km on a single charge.  

Key technical details: each BEMU will use a 800 V high‑power battery architecture, enabling fast charging at 1 MW from dedicated wayside chargers installed at major termini. The packs employ a nickel‑cobalt‑manganese (NCM 811) cathode chemistry with a silicon‑graphite anode, targeting an energy density of 260 Wh kg⁻¹ at the pack level. A regenerative‑braking system is expected to recover up to 30 % of kinetic energy during deceleration, extending operational range and reducing overall energy consumption by an estimated 15 % compared with diesel equivalents.  

The programme aims to retire approximately 300 diesel multiple units by 2030, cutting CO₂ emissions by an estimated 1.2 Mt CO₂e per year. It also creates a new market for high‑power battery packs in the rail sector, a segment that has historically relied on lead‑acid or nickel‑metal‑hydride solutions. By standardising a 800 V platform, the DfT hopes to achieve economies of scale that will bring pack costs down to below £150 kWh, a price point that could make battery‑electric rail competitive with electrified overhead‑line systems on routes where full electrification is economically prohibitive.  

The announcement underscores a broader shift in Europe toward zero‑emission rail, complementing similar initiatives in Germany and France. It also signals growing confidence in large‑format lithium‑ion technology for high‑power, high‑cycle‑life applications beyond automotive, potentially opening pathways for future battery‑electric freight locomotives.

## 03 BYD Denza N8L Rolls Out with 960 km Range on a 150 kWh LFP Pack  

Chinese automaker BYD’s premium sub‑brand Denza launched the all‑electric N8L sedan on 14 September 2026, boasting a claimed 960 km (NEDC) driving range [CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2026/09/14/byd-denza-launches-all-electric-n8l/) . The vehicle is powered by a 150 kWh lithium‑iron‑phosphate (LFP) battery pack that uses BYD’s proprietary “Blade” cell architecture, which arranges flat cells in a single‑direction layout to maximise volumetric efficiency.  

The Blade pack delivers an energy density of 210 Wh L⁻¹ at the pack level—higher than conventional LFP packs—while retaining the intrinsic safety benefits of iron‑phosphate chemistry, such as resistance to thermal runaway. Fast‑charging capability is rated at 800 V, 350 kW, allowing the N8L to recover 80 % of its capacity in roughly 25 minutes on a compatible DC charger. BYD also integrated an on‑board battery‑management system (BMS) that actively balances cells and monitors temperature across 96 modules, extending cycle life to an estimated 2,000 full‑depth‑of‑discharge cycles.  

From a market perspective, the N8L targets the upper‑mid‑size sedan segment in China and emerging markets, where consumers are increasingly demanding long‑range EVs without the premium price tag of nickel‑cobalt chemistries. BYD’s decision to stick with LFP—despite its lower specific energy compared with NCM—reflects a strategic move to reduce reliance on imported nickel and cobalt, aligning with China’s broader resource‑security goals.  

The launch also has implications for the global battery supply chain. BYD’s Blade LFP cells are produced in its vertically integrated plant in Xi’an, which has a current annual capacity of 45 GWh. Scaling the N8L’s 150 kWh pack could push that facility toward its 2028 target of 70 GWh, potentially reshaping LFP market dynamics and putting pressure on competitors such as CATL and BYD’s own rivals to improve LFP performance.  

Overall, the N8L demonstrates that LFP technology can now meet premium‑segment range expectations, challenging the long‑standing perception that only nickel‑rich chemistries can deliver near‑1000 km ranges. Its success could accelerate the adoption of safer, cheaper LFP packs across a broader range of vehicle classes worldwide.
