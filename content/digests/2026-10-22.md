## 01 Tesla Cites Battery Supply Shortage as Primary Brake on Q3 Vehicle Sales

Tesla’s Q3 2026 shareholder letter, released on 21 October, placed the spotlight on battery cell availability as the chief obstacle to higher vehicle deliveries. The automaker reported a 5 % year‑over‑year decline in total vehicle sales, attributing the shortfall not to waning consumer demand but to “limited battery supply” that constrained production across its Model Y, Model 3, and upcoming Cybertruck lines. Tesla disclosed that its battery backlog grew to 1.2 million units—the largest in four years—while average selling prices continued to fall, with the Model Y price dropping 3 % in the quarter.

The letter also revealed that Tesla’s in‑house cell production at its Nevada Gigafactory (partnered with Panasonic) is operating at 85 % of capacity, still short of the 150 GWh annual target set for 2025. To bridge the gap, the company is accelerating the rollout of its new 4680 cells, but pilot lines are not expected to reach full volume until 2027. Meanwhile, external suppliers such as CATL and LG Energy Solution have reported supply chain constraints linked to raw‑material shortages (nickel, cobalt) and geopolitical trade curbs.

Analysts view the admission as a warning sign for the broader EV market, where many OEMs rely on a thin global battery supply chain. If Tesla’s bottleneck persists, it could pressure other manufacturers to secure additional cell capacity or accelerate solid‑state development. The situation also underscores the strategic importance of expanding domestic cell production in the United States and Europe.

## 02 GM Announces Closure of Brownstown Battery Plant, Shifting Production to Tennessee

General Motors confirmed on 21 October that its Brownstown, Michigan battery facility will be closed by early 2027. The plant, which began operations in 2022 to produce lithium‑ion cells for the Cadillac Celestiq luxury sedan and the Chevrolet Corvette E‑Ray powertrain, will see its remaining workforce of roughly 800 employees reassigned to other GM sites or offered severance packages.

GM’s decision follows the company’s broader “EV Platform 4.0” strategy, which emphasizes a leaner battery supply chain centered on its partnership with LG Energy Solution at the Ultium Cells plant in Spring Hill, Tennessee. The Tennessee site is slated to begin production of lithium‑manganese‑rich (LMR) prismatic cells later this year, a chemistry that promises higher energy density and lower cobalt usage. By consolidating cell output in Tennessee, GM aims to cut logistics costs and improve volume flexibility for its upcoming electric SUVs and trucks.

The closure also reflects shifting market dynamics: demand for high‑end, low‑volume luxury EVs has softened, while mass‑market models require larger, more cost‑effective cell formats. Industry observers note that the Brownstown shutdown may accelerate the industry’s move away from small‑batch, high‑cost cell lines toward larger, standardized production hubs.

Local officials in Michigan have expressed concern over the loss of high‑tech jobs, but GM pledged to invest in workforce retraining programs in partnership with community colleges. The move underscores the accelerating consolidation of battery manufacturing in regions with established supply‑chain ecosystems and favorable policy incentives.

## 03 Ultium Cells Starts LMR Prismatic Cell Production in Tennessee for Next‑Gen GM EVs

Ultium Cells LLC, the joint venture between General Motors and LG Energy Solution, announced on 19 October that its Spring Hill, Tennessee facility will commence production of lithium‑manganese‑rich (LMR) prismatic battery cells in Q4 2026. The new line is designed to supply GM’s upcoming fourth‑generation EV platform, which targets a 30 % reduction in battery pack weight and a 20 % cost drop relative to current models.

The LMR chemistry blends higher manganese content with a reduced cobalt fraction, delivering an energy density of approximately 250 Wh/kg—about 10 % higher than the NMC‑811 cells currently in GM’s lineup. Initial annual capacity is projected at 30 GWh, with a planned expansion to 70 GWh by 2029, enough to power roughly 1 million electric vehicles per year.

Ultium Cells’ Tennessee plant will integrate advanced dry‑coating technology and a fully automated cell‑stacking line, cutting labor costs and improving yield. The venture also benefits from a $1.2 billion incentive package from the state of Tennessee, which includes tax credits for renewable‑energy usage and workforce development grants.

Strategically, the move positions GM to reduce its reliance on overseas cell suppliers and to meet the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act’s domestic content requirements. Analysts expect the LMR cells to be used in GM’s upcoming electric pickups and SUVs, where higher energy density translates into longer range without enlarging the vehicle footprint.

The announcement follows earlier news that GM is closing its Brownstown battery plant, consolidating cell production in Tennessee to achieve economies of scale and a more resilient supply chain.
