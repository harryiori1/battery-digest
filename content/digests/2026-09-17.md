## 01 Factorial Energy Partners with Mitsui Kinzoku to Scale Sulfide Solid Electrolytes
Factorial Energy, a U.S. solid‑state battery start‑up, announced a strategic partnership with Mitsui Kinzoku, one of the few global producers of sulfide‑based solid electrolytes. The agreement secures a multi‑year supply of high‑purity Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂ (LGPS) electrolyte, the material Factorial has identified as the most promising for its “Lithion” solid‑state cell architecture. Under the deal, Mitsui will deliver up to 5 GWh of electrolyte per year, enough to support pilot‑scale production of Factorial’s 300 Wh/kg cells by 2028.

Factorial’s “Lithion” cells replace the conventional liquid electrolyte with a thin sulfide layer, promising higher energy density, faster charge rates, and improved safety. The company claims its prototype cells have already demonstrated 350 Wh/kg at 0 °C and can be charged to 80 % in under 10 minutes. Scaling the electrolyte supply is the last major bottleneck; Mitsui’s expertise in large‑scale sulfide synthesis will allow Factorial to move from lab‑scale to a 10 GWh annual output line in its planned Arizona facility.

The partnership also includes joint R&D on interface engineering to mitigate dendrite formation—a key challenge for sulfide electrolytes. Both parties will co‑invest $120 million in a new pilot line slated for completion in Q4 2027. Industry analysts see the deal as a watershed moment for solid‑state commercialization, moving the technology from niche applications (e.g., aerospace) toward mass‑market EVs within the next five years. If successful, Factorial could challenge incumbent lithium‑ion manufacturers by offering a safer, higher‑energy alternative that fits existing vehicle platforms.

## 02 BYD Plans Three European Assembly Plants and One Dedicated Battery Factory
Chinese EV giant BYD disclosed a comprehensive European expansion blueprint that calls for three vehicle assembly plants and a dedicated battery manufacturing facility by 2032. The plan, outlined by BYD’s European head office in a briefing to Chinese trade officials, targets a combined annual output of 1.2 million vehicles and 150 GWh of battery cells.

The first assembly plant is slated for Hungary’s Győr region, leveraging existing automotive infrastructure and a skilled workforce. A second site is under negotiation in Spain’s Valencia province, while the third will be located in Poland’s Silesian corridor, a hub for logistics and parts suppliers. The battery factory, proposed for the same Hungarian industrial park, will employ a “cell‑to‑pack” production line based on BYD’s Blade battery technology, which uses a thin, LFP‑based cell design that eliminates the need for a traditional module structure. BYD claims the Blade system can achieve a 15 % increase in pack energy density while reducing weight by 10 % compared with conventional LFP packs.

The European rollout aligns with the EU’s “Fit for 55” climate package, which incentivizes local battery production through subsidies and tax breaks. BYD expects the battery plant to secure a 30 % share of the EU’s projected 2027‑2030 battery demand, positioning the company as the region’s second‑largest battery maker after CATL. The investment, estimated at €4.5 billion, will create roughly 8,000 jobs across the four sites and include a training academy focused on advanced battery manufacturing and recycling.

Analysts note that BYD’s move addresses two critical pain points for European OEMs: supply‑chain resilience and cost pressure from raw‑material price volatility. By localizing both vehicle assembly and battery production, BYD can reduce logistics costs, shorten lead times, and better comply with upcoming EU battery‑passport regulations. The strategy also signals BYD’s confidence that its LFP‑centric approach can compete with nickel‑rich chemistries in the European market, especially as stricter sustainability standards favor the lower‑impact LFP chemistry.

## 03 Zhongqi New Energy and Leapmotor Sign Joint‑Development Pact for Next‑Gen Battery Cells
Zhongqi New Energy (ZQNE) and Leapmotor announced a joint‑development agreement aimed at creating a new generation of high‑energy‑density battery cells and establishing a standardized cell format for mass production. The memorandum of understanding, signed in Shanghai on 15 September, outlines a three‑year collaboration that will combine Zhongqi’s expertise in advanced cathode materials with Leapmotor’s in‑house cell‑design capabilities.

The partnership’s first technical milestone is the development of a 5 Ah, 4.8 V lithium‑nickel‑cobalt‑manganese‑oxide (NCM) cell that incorporates a silicon‑graphite composite anode. Preliminary lab results suggest the cell can achieve an energy density of 280 Wh/kg—approximately 20 % higher than Leapmotor’s current 230 Wh/kg cells—while maintaining a cycle life of over 1,200 full‑charge cycles at 80 % depth of discharge. To ensure manufacturability, the duo will adopt a “tab‑less” electrode architecture that reduces internal resistance and simplifies the welding process.

A key component of the pact is the creation of a standardized 18650‑compatible “SmartCell” format, which integrates a built‑in battery‑management microcontroller for real‑time health monitoring. This standardization is intended to lower R&D costs for downstream OEMs and accelerate the rollout of next‑generation EVs across China’s rapidly expanding market.

Financially, Leapmotor will invest ¥1.2 billion (≈ US$170 million) in a pilot line at Zhongqi’s Jiangsu facility, with production capacity slated for 30 GWh by 2029. The agreement also includes joint patents, with revenue sharing based on a 60/40 split favoring Zhongqi for material innovations and Leapmotor for cell‑design breakthroughs.

Industry observers view the collaboration as a strategic response to the looming raw‑material constraints on nickel and cobalt, as well as a bid to close the performance gap with solid‑state technologies. By focusing on silicon‑anode integration and cell‑level intelligence, the partnership could set a new benchmark for cost‑effective, high‑energy EV batteries in the mid‑term.
